The resume format taught by Harvard's Office of Career Services is deliberately plain: a single column, one typeface at one size, no colour, no graphics, no columns, no photo. Your name at the top, contact details beneath it, then Education and Experience in reverse chronological order, each entry a line of employer and location, a line of title and dates, and bullets underneath.

It looks unfinished next to the templates sold online. That is the point, and it is why it survives both machines and skim-reading humans better than almost anything else.

## What the format actually specifies

The details vary between the versions floating around, but the load-bearing rules are consistent:

- **One column.** Nothing beside anything else.
- **One typeface, one size** for body text. Bold and italic carry the hierarchy — bold for employers and titles, italic sparingly.
- **No colour, no rules, no icons, no photo.** No skill bars, no rating dots.
- **Reverse chronological**, most recent first, in every section.
- **Employer and location on one line; title and dates on the next**, right-aligned dates.
- **Bullets that are achievements, not duties**, each starting with a verb.
- **One page** for most people; two once you have the history to justify it.
- **Standard section names** — Education, Experience, Skills. Not "My Journey".

Harvard publishes its own guidance and templates through its career office, and other university career centres teach something almost identical. There is no single canonical file, so treat the rules above as the format rather than any one document as the format.

## Why it beats designed templates at the thing that matters

A resume has to survive two very different readers, and the plain format is the only one that is good for both.

**The parser.** An applicant tracking system converts your file into fields — employer, title, dates, education — and it is guessing from layout. A single column has exactly one reading order, so there is nothing to guess. Two columns do not: a sidebar of skills next to a column of jobs can come out interleaved line by line, and your work history arrives shredded. Tables, text boxes and text-inside-images fail in their own ways. There is [more on what parsers actually do](/resources/what-an-ats-actually-does/), but the short version is that layout breaks resumes far more often than wording does.

**The human.** A recruiter with 200 applications gives the first pass a few seconds. They are looking for where you worked, what you did, and how long. The plain format puts all three in the same place on every resume, which is exactly what someone comparing candidates wants. A designed template makes them hunt, and being visually distinctive is not an advantage when the reader is trying to answer three questions quickly.

The counter-argument — that a plain document is forgettable — assumes the resume is the thing being judged. It usually is not. It is the thing that gets you to the conversation where you are judged.

## Where designed resumes are genuinely fine

Not everywhere is the same. If you are applying for a visual design or brand role, a portfolio-grade document is part of the work sample, and a plain one may undersell you. If you are applying through a personal introduction and you know a human is opening the file directly, parsing does not apply.

Everywhere else — and that is most applications, most of the time — the plain format costs you nothing and protects you from a failure mode you would never find out about. Nobody emails to say your PDF parsed badly.

## Checking your own document in thirty seconds

Open your resume, select all, copy, paste into a plain text editor.

What you are looking at is roughly what the software gets. Read it as a stranger would:

- Are the employers still attached to the right dates?
- Did any section disappear?
- Is your email address there, or was it in the page header?
- Does the order make sense, or is a sidebar interleaved through your job history?

If that text reads cleanly, your layout is fine regardless of how it looks. If it does not, no amount of keyword tuning will help, because the recruiter's search is running against the mess rather than the document.

## The format is easy. The material is not

Getting the layout right takes an afternoon. Writing bullets worth putting in it is the harder half, and it depends on something you had to do years earlier: writing down what actually happened, with the numbers, while you still had access to them.

Most resumes are thinner than the careers behind them for that reason — not because people cannot write, but because by the time they sit down to it the evidence is gone. [Getting the bullets right](/resources/star-method-resume-bullets/) covers what a strong one looks like and where the material has to come from.
