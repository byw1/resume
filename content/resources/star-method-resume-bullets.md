STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result — was built for answering behavioural interview questions out loud. You get sixty to ninety seconds, the interviewer can interrupt, and the structure keeps you from rambling. It is genuinely good at that.

A resume bullet is one or two lines. Roughly fourteen to twenty-five words. If you spend the first half on Situation and Task, the Action arrives late and the Result gets cut. That is why so much STAR advice produces bullets that read like throat-clearing.

The fix is not to abandon the thinking. It is to keep the parts that carry information and compress the rest.

## What each letter is actually worth on paper

**Situation** is context. On a resume, most of it is already implied by the job title and the company, which sit directly above the bullet. A reader who knows you were Director of Operations at a freight company does not need a sentence establishing that the company moved freight.

**Task** is what you were asked to do. Nearly always redundant on a resume: your title said it. "I was responsible for" is the purest form of this waste.

**Action** is what you did. This is the load-bearing part and it should open the bullet.

**Result** is what changed. This is what the reader is actually scanning for, and it is the part people most often leave out.

So the resume form of STAR is closer to **Action, Result, with context compressed into a clause** — and only when the context is not obvious.

## Three rewrites

**Before.** *"In my role as Operations Manager, I was tasked with addressing inefficiencies in our regional dispatch structure. I led a project to consolidate the desks, working with stakeholders across the business, which resulted in significant cost savings."*

Forty-one words. The first twenty say nothing the job title has not said. "Significant" is doing the work a number should do.

**After.** *"Consolidated four regional dispatch desks into one, saving $2.1M a year."*

Eleven words. Action first, result last, context carried by "four regional dispatch desks".

---

**Before.** *"Responsible for improving on-time delivery performance across the carrier network through a variety of process improvements and vendor management initiatives."*

No result at all. It describes a job description, not a person.

**After.** *"Took on-time pickup from 82% to 96% over six quarters by rebuilding the carrier scorecard."*

The method moved to the end, where it supports the number instead of delaying it.

---

**Before.** *"Situation: our platform rollout was behind schedule. Task: I was asked to get it back on track. Action: I restructured the delivery plan across three regions. Result: we launched two days early."*

Someone has followed the framework literally. It reads like a form.

**After.** *"Delivered a three-region platform rollout two days ahead of schedule, with churn flat through the transition."*

Same facts. One sentence. The "churn flat" clause is the part a good hiring manager will stop on, because it says you did not buy the date with something else.

## The pattern underneath

Strong bullets tend to look like:

> **[Action verb] [what, specifically] [result with a number] [qualifier that pre-empts the obvious objection].**

That last clause is the one most people miss and the one that most distinguishes a real bullet from a generated one. "Saving $2.1M a year" invites the question *at what cost*. "With churn flat through the transition" answers it before it is asked.

A few rules that hold up:

- **Open with a verb, in the past tense**, except for the job you currently hold.
- **One number per bullet, minimum.** If you genuinely have none, the bullet is probably describing a duty rather than an achievement — cut it or find the outcome.
- **Percentages and absolutes together** where you can. "Cut late deliveries 31% across 40 lanes" is stronger than either half.
- **No "responsible for", no "helped to", no "worked on".** They signal proximity to work rather than ownership of it.
- **Do not repeat the job title** in the bullet. It is directly above.

## The part nobody tells you: you cannot write these from memory

Every rewrite above turns on a specific number. $2.1M. 82% to 96%. Six quarters. Two days early. Churn flat.

Nobody remembers those two years later. They existed in a dashboard you no longer have access to, a deck you cannot find, or a Slack message in a workspace you were deprovisioned from. So when you sit down to write the resume, you have the shape of the achievement and none of the evidence, and you write "significant cost savings" — not because you are lazy, but because the number is gone.

This is the real reason resumes get thinner than the careers behind them. It is not a writing problem. It is a record-keeping problem that only presents itself as a writing problem, years later, at the exact moment it is too late to fix.

The habit that solves it is unglamorous: when something goes well, write down what happened and the number, that week, somewhere durable. Two sentences. You are not writing a resume — you are leaving evidence for the person who will have to.

That is the whole idea behind [Hired](/): a place to put those two sentences the week they happen, so the resume gets written out of what you actually did rather than what you can still recall. It also means nothing on the document is invented, because everything on it can be traced back to something you wrote when you still knew it was true.

## Where STAR still belongs

In the interview. When someone says "tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult stakeholder", the full structure is exactly right — you have the time, the listener needs the context, and the arc keeps you from wandering.

Use STAR to prepare what you will say. Use Action-plus-Result to write what they will read.
